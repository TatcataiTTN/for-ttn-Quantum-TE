From the physical impulse to the symbol \delta

Complication: Why use something that does not exist, and what does "a function infinite at the origin" mean?
Resolution: Treat the impulse as shorthand for a brief unit-area pulse whose detailed shape cannot be measured.
  • The impulse is about the integral, not the shape

1/5 · From the physical impulse to the symbol \delta

The impulse: only the integral matters

We need notation for intense unit-area pulses so brief that measuring equipment of a given resolving power cannot tell them from even briefer ones. Mechanics calls this an "impulse". The important attribute is its integral; the precise details of its form are unimportant (Bracewell, p. 74).

1/5 · From the physical impulse to the symbol \delta

History of the symbol \delta(x)

The idea has been current for a century or more in mathematical circles: van der Pol and Bremmer (1955) cite examples from Hermite, Cauchy, Poisson, Kirchhoff, Helmholtz, Kelvin and Heaviside. The notation \delta(x) was first used by G. Kirchhoff and introduced into quantum mechanics by Dirac in 1927 (p. 74).

1/5 · From the physical impulse to the symbol \delta

Things that do not exist but are useful

Point masses, point charges, point sources, concentrated forces, line sources and surface charges are familiar and accepted entities in physics, though they do not exist. Their conceptual value stems from the fact that the impulse response, the effect associated with the impulse, may be indistinguishable, given measuring equipment of specified resolving power, from the response due to a physically realisable pulse (p. 74).

1/5 · From the physical impulse to the symbol \delta

Definition: not a function

We wish to write \delta(x)=0 for x\ne0 and \int\delta=1, but the impulse symbol does not represent a function in the sense of analysis. Dirac called it an "improper function", and the integral above has no meaning until a convention of interpretation is declared. Here it means the limit of \int\tau^{-1}\Pi(x/\tau)dx as \tau\to0 (pp. 74 to 75).

The interpretation
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x)\,dx\ \equiv\ \lim_{\tau\to0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tau^{-1}\Pi\!\left(\frac{x}{\tau}\right)dx=1
  • \tau^{-1}\Pi(x/\tau)rectangle of height \tau^{-1}, base \tau, unit area
1/5 · From the physical impulse to the symbol \delta

An RC low-pass circuit: the pulse shape gradually stops mattering

Feed voltage pulses into a low-pass filter: the transient response settles to a definite form as the pulses shorten, independent of the input pulse shape, because the high-frequency components that distinguish the pulses produce negligible response (p. 75). For an RC circuit with h(t)=e^{-t}H(t) and three unit-area pulses (rectangle, triangle, two small pulses) of width \tau: at t=1 the spread among the three responses is 0.009 for \tau=0.5, 0.0003 for \tau=0.1 and 0 for \tau=0.01.

1/5 · From the physical impulse to the symbol \delta

The relation between \delta and the step

Since \int_{-\infty}^x\delta(x')dx' is 1 for x>0 and 0 for x<0, we have \int_{-\infty}^x\delta=H(x). To interpret it, replace \delta by the sequence \tau^{-1}\Pi(x/\tau): the integrals are ramp-step functions (Fig. 5.1); fix x and let \tau\to0 (pp. 75 to 77). At x=0.1: \tau=0.5 gives 0.7, \tau=0.1 gives 1; at x=-0.1: 0.3 and 0.

Derivative of the step
\int_{-\infty}^{x}\delta(x')\,dx'=H(x),\qquad \delta(x)=\frac{d}{dx}H(x)
1/5 · From the physical impulse to the symbol \delta

What "the derivative of the step is the impulse" means

The step has no derivative at the origin, so the statement is shorthand for: the derivatives of a sequence of differentiable functions approaching H(x) constitute a suitable defining sequence for \delta(x). The ramp-step functions of Fig. 5.1 are differentiable and approach H; since the step is always 1 the area under each derivative is 1, qualifying it as a unit-impulse sequence (p. 77).

1/5 · From the physical impulse to the symbol \delta

Many different sequences define the same \delta

Bracewell lists sequences as \tau approaches zero through positive values (pp. 77 to 78): a displaced rectangle (left edge at the origin, common in switched circuits), the Gaussian (derivatives of all orders exist), the triangle (continuous, zero outside |x|<\tau), \tau^{-1}\text{sinc}(x/\tau) (does not die out yet serves), the resonance profile \tau/[\pi(x^2+\tau^2)] (decays slowly), and a smooth compactly supported bump. The table gives \int f\,\delta_\tau\,dx with f=\cos x+\tfrac12\sin2x (so f(0)=1):

Sifting, scaling and the shah train

Situation: To compute with \delta like an ordinary function we need exact rules.
Complication: A few seemingly obvious rules have traps: \delta(ax), x\delta(x), \text{III}(ax).
Resolution: Sifting is the central rule and shah extends it to a regular train of impulses.
  • Sifting and convolution with \delta
  • Scaling \delta(ax) and the qualified x\delta(x)=0
  • The shah train: sampling and replication
2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

The sifting property

Replace \delta by \tau^{-1}\Pi(x/\tau), multiply by f, integrate and let \tau\to0: the area is about \tau^{-1} times the shaded part, whose average height is near f(0) and width \tau, so it tends to f(0). The operation "sifts" out a single value of f. The pulse shape does not matter, only its integral counts (p. 79).

Sifting property
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x)f(x)\,dx=f(0),\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x-a)f(x)\,dx=f(a)
2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

Convolution with \delta changes nothing

Written as a convolution, \delta(x)*f(x)=f(x)*\delta(x)=f(x). If f has a jump at x=0 the sifting integral has limiting value \tfrac12[f(0+)+f(0-)], so more generally \delta*f=\tfrac12[f(x+)+f(x-)]; it differs from f only by a null function (p. 80). Measured with f=e^{-x}H(x): the centred sequence gives 0.5, the left-edge sequence gives 1 at the jump.

Convolution with the impulse
\delta*f=f*\delta=\tfrac12\left[f(x^+)+f(x^-)\right]
2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

The left-edge sequence sifts f(x+)

The asymmetric rectangular sequence \tau^{-1}\Pi[(x-\tau/2)/\tau] sifts out f(x+). At points of discontinuity this sequence gives a different result. In transient analysis, where discontinuities at the switching instant t=0 are particularly common, the choice of sequence can appear to give different answers, but the difference can only be instantaneous (p. 80).

2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

Scaling: \delta(ax)=|a|^{-1}\delta(x)

Compressing the scale of x by a reduces the area of pulses that previously had unit area, so the strength of the impulse is reduced by |a|. The modulus allows for \delta(-x)=\delta(x) (p. 80). Measured: \int f\,\tau^{-1}\Pi(3x/\tau)dx with \tau=0.01 equals 0.3333, close to f(0)/3.

Scaling
\delta(ax)=\frac{1}{|a|}\,\delta(x),\qquad \delta(-x)=\delta(x)
2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

Multiplying by a continuous function: f(x)\delta(x)=f(0)\delta(x)

If f is continuous at 0 then f(x)\delta(x)=f(0)\delta(x) (p. 81). Numerical check with the test function g=e^{-x^2} and a Gaussian sequence \tau=0.01: \int f\delta_\tau g\,dx = 1, exactly f(0)g(0)=1.

2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

x\delta(x)=0: true, but qualified

From sifting with f=x, \int x\delta(x)dx=0, and one writes x\delta(x)=0. But contemplating the prelimit graphs, this equation conceals a nonvanishing component reminiscent of the Gibbs phenomenon: \lim_{\tau\to0}[x\tau^{-1}\Pi(x/\tau)]=0 for all x, yet its maximum is always \tfrac12 and its minimum -\tfrac12 (p. 81). Measured: maximum 0.5 for both \tau=0.1 and \tau=0.001.

2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

The shah impulse train \text{III}(x)

An infinite sequence of unit impulses spaced by 1 (Fig. 5.4). Every reservation about \delta applies, and more: infinitely many infinite discontinuities and a nonconvergent infinite integral, so all conditions for a Fourier transform are violated; yet it is extremely useful and easy to manipulate (pp. 81 to 82). Obvious properties: period 1, even, \text{III}(x-\tfrac12)=\text{III}(x+\tfrac12).

Shah
\text{III}(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x-n),\qquad \text{III}(-x)=\text{III}(x),\qquad \text{III}(x+n)=\text{III}(x)
2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

Sampling: multiplying by shah

Multiplying f(x) by \text{III}(x) samples it at unit intervals: \text{III}(x)f(x)=\sum f(n)\delta(x-n). The information between the integers is lost but the values at integers are preserved. Shah serves antenna-array patterns, diffraction gratings, raster scanning in television and radar, pulse modulation, data sampling, Fourier series and tabular computing (p. 82). The sum of samples \sum_nf(n) for f=e^{-\pi x^2} is 1.0864.

Sampling
\text{III}(x)\,f(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}f(n)\,\delta(x-n)
2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

Replication: convolving with shah

\text{III}*f=\sum f(x-n): f appears in replica at unit intervals on both sides; if f is more than one unit wide the replicas overlap (Fig. 5.6). This twofold character is no accident: \text{III} is its own Fourier transform (in the limit), making it twice as useful (pp. 82 to 83). For f=e^{-\pi x^2}, (\text{III}*f)(0.5) = 0.9136, computed both by the direct sum and by the Fourier series \sum(-1)^ke^{-\pi k^2}.

Replication
\text{III}*f(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}f(x-n)
2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

The \text{III}(ax) trap: impulse strength

Squeezing shah by 2 into \text{III}(2x) packs the impulses twice as closely, but for algebraic consistency their strength drops by the same factor: \text{III}(ax)=|a|^{-1}\sum\delta(x-n/a). If it is stretched so that the spacing is X then \text{III}(x/X) does not have unit strength: unit impulses spaced X are X^{-1}\text{III}(x/X) (p. 83). Measured with f=e^{-\pi x^2} (area 1): \sum f(nX) = 1.0864 (X=1) and 2 (X=0.5, twice too large because the factor X is missing), while X\sum f(nX) = 1 (X=0.5), the area.

Shah with spacing X
\text{III}(ax)=\frac1{|a|}\sum_n\delta\!\left(x-\frac na\right),\qquad \text{unit impulses spaced }X:\ X^{-1}\,\text{III}(x/X)
2/5 · Sifting, scaling and the shah train

Poisson duality: shah is self-transforming

Since \text{III}\supset\text{III}, the sum of samples of f equals the sum of samples of F: \sum f(n)=\sum F(k). With f=e^{-\pi x^2/4} having F=2e^{-4\pi s^2}: the left side is 2.000014 and the right side 2.000014, equal to rounding error. This is the Poisson summation formula chapter 10 will use for the sampling theorem.

Poisson summation
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}f(n)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}F(k)

3/5 · Even and odd impulse pairs and derivatives of \delta

The even and odd impulse pairs

Bracewell defines (Fig. 5.7, special symbols in Table 4.1; written here as \mu and \nu):

Even and odd pairs
\mu(x)=\tfrac12\delta\!\left(x+\tfrac12\right)+\tfrac12\delta\!\left(x-\tfrac12\right),\qquad \nu(x)=\tfrac12\delta\!\left(x+\tfrac12\right)-\tfrac12\delta\!\left(x-\tfrac12\right)

They matter because of their transform relation to cosine and sine: \mu(x)\supset\cos\pi s, \cos\pi x\supset\mu(s), and \nu(x)\supset i\sin\pi s, \sin\pi x\supset i\nu(s) (p. 84). Notebook: the numerical transform of \mu at s=0.3 is 0.5878, exactly \cos0.3\pi.

3/5 · Even and odd impulse pairs and derivatives of \delta

The even pair duplicates a function

Convolving with \mu gives \mu*f=\tfrac12f(x+\tfrac12)+\tfrac12f(x-\tfrac12), the mean of two displaced copies (Fig. 5.8). Normalising to unit area has advantages; occasionally two unit impulses would be preferable (p. 84). With f=e^{-\pi x^2} at x=0: 0.4559.

3/5 · Even and odd impulse pairs and derivatives of \delta

Finite difference through the odd pair

Defining the finite difference \Delta f(x)=f(x+\tfrac12)-f(x-\tfrac12) we have \Delta f=2\nu*f, i.e. the operator \Delta=2\nu* (pp. 84 to 85). Numerical check: for f=\sin x, \Delta f(0)=2\sin\tfrac12 = 0.9589; for f=e^{-\pi x^2} the transform of \Delta f is 2i\sin(\pi s)F(s), of magnitude 1.2195 at s=0.3 by both numerical integration and the formula.

Finite difference
\Delta f(x)=f\!\left(x+\tfrac12\right)-f\!\left(x-\tfrac12\right)=2\,\nu(x)*f(x)
3/5 · Even and odd impulse pairs and derivatives of \delta

The first derivative \delta'(x): the dipole

\delta'(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\delta(x) evokes an infinitesimal dipole in electrostatics, a familiar and easy picture. The notation \delta' carries that facility into mathematics, but we cannot ask a function to go positively infinite just left of the origin, negatively infinite just right, and zero everywhere else. We would also wish to write \delta'(0)=0 (p. 85).

3/5 · Even and odd impulse pairs and derivatives of \delta

Rigorous reading: derivatives of a pulse sequence

We fall back on pulse sequences as for \delta and consider their derivatives (Fig. 5.9). For example \int\delta'(x)dx=0 is shorthand for \lim\int[\text{derivative of the pulse}]dx=0. The exact pulse form is unimportant, even a rectangle will do, but in later work a smooth pulse may offer an advantage (pp. 85 to 86). Derivative sifting: \int\delta'(x)f(x)dx=-f'(0); with the Gaussian sequence \tau=0.05 and f=\cos x+\tfrac12\sin2x it gives -0.9992, equal to -\int\delta_\tau f' (difference 6.7e-16).

Derivative sifting
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta'(x)\,f(x)\,dx=-f'(0)
3/5 · Even and odd impulse pairs and derivatives of \delta

Properties of \delta'

The accompanying properties (p. 86): \int x\delta'(x)dx=-1, \int|\delta'(x)|dx=\infty, \delta'(-x)=-\delta'(x), x\delta'(x)=-\delta(x), and f(x)\delta'(x)=f(0)\delta'(x)-f'(0)\delta(x). Measured: \int x\delta'_\tau dx = -1; \int|\delta'_\tau|dx = 20 for \tau=0.1 and 200 for \tau=0.01, growing like 2/\tau: no finite limit.

A few relations
x\,\delta'(x)=-\delta(x),\qquad f(x)\,\delta'(x)=f(0)\,\delta'(x)-f'(0)\,\delta(x)
3/5 · Even and odd impulse pairs and derivatives of \delta

Higher derivatives

For higher-order derivatives (p. 87): \int\delta''(x)dx=0, \int x^2\delta''(x)dx=2, x^n\delta^{(n)}(x)=(-1)^nn!\,\delta(x), and \int\delta^{(n)}(x)f(x)dx=(-1)^nf^{(n)}(0). Measured: \int x^2\delta''_\tau dx = 2; with f=\cos x+\tfrac12\sin2x, \int\delta''f = -1, exactly f''(0)=-1.

n-th derivative sifting
\int\delta^{(n)}(x)\,f(x)\,dx=(-1)^n f^{(n)}(0)
3/5 · Even and odd impulse pairs and derivatives of \delta

Null functions

Null functions are known chiefly for having Fourier transforms that are zero while not themselves identically zero. By definition f is null if \int_a^bf\,dx=0 for all a,b. Lerch's theorem: if two functions have the same transform then their difference is a null function. An example is \delta^{\circ}(x), 0 for x\ne0 and 1 at x=0; it describes the current taken by a series combination of a resistance and a capacitance from a battery, as the capacitance approaches zero (p. 87). Measured: the area of a single point of height 1 on a grid of step 10^{-3} and 10^{-6} is 1e-03 and 1e-06, tending to 0.

Step and null function
H(x)=\hat H(x)+\tfrac12\,\delta^{\circ}(x)

4/5 · Multidimensional impulses

Two- and three-dimensional impulses

^2\delta(x,y) describes the pressure distribution over the plane when a concentrated unit force is applied at the origin; ^3\delta(x,y,z) describes the charge density in a volume containing a unit charge at (0,0,0). Properties are established with sequences such as \tau^{-2}\Pi(x/\tau)\Pi(y/\tau) of unit volume (p. 89).

Two-dimensional impulse
\iint{}^2\delta(x,y)\,dx\,dy=1,\qquad {}^2\delta(ax,by)=\frac{1}{|ab|}\,{}^2\delta(x,y),\qquad {}^2\delta(x,y)=\delta(x)\,\delta(y)
4/5 · Multidimensional impulses

Radial and cylindrical forms

With r^2=x^2+y^2, ^2\delta(x,y)=\delta(r)/\pi r. In three dimensions, ^3\delta(x,y,z)=\delta(x)\delta(y)\delta(z)={}^2\delta(x,y)\delta(z), with matching forms in cylindrical and spherical coordinates (pp. 89 to 90). Measured: the integral over the plane (2\pi r\,dr) of a two-dimensional Gaussian sequence (\tau=0.1) equals 1.

4/5 · Multidimensional impulses

The bed of nails ^2\text{III}(x,y)

For two-dimensional arrays use the bed-of-nails symbol (Fig. 5.12). It factorises: ^2\text{III}(x,y)=\text{III}(x)\text{III}(y), is doubly periodic, and f(x,y)\,{}^2\text{III}=\sum\sum f(m,n)\,{}^2\delta(x-m,y-n): two-dimensionally sampled data and the coefficients of double Fourier series (pp. 90 to 91). Convolving with it gives two-dimensional replicas, as in an array of identical antennas. Measured: \sum\sum e^{-\pi(m^2+n^2)} = 1.1803, the square of the one-dimensional sum 1.0864.

Bed of nails
{}^2\text{III}(x,y)=\sum_{m}\sum_{n}{}^2\delta(x-m,\,y-n)=\text{III}(x)\,\text{III}(y)
4/5 · Multidimensional impulses

The row of spikes and the grating

Two other important two-dimensional distributions need no new symbols: the row of spikes \text{III}(x)\delta(y) and the grating \text{III}(x) (Fig. 5.14). They form a two-dimensional Fourier transform pair, suitable for discussing the diffraction of light by a row of pinholes or by a diffraction grating (p. 91).

4/5 · Multidimensional impulses

Derivatives of impulses: dipoles and quadrupoles

Interpreted with derivatives of impulses, many forms are familiar electric-charge distributions. \delta'(x) on the (x,y)-plane is a line distribution of dipole moment on the y-axis; in three dimensions a sheet of dipole moment on the (y,z)-plane. A simple dipole at the origin with unit moment along x is -\delta'(x)\delta(y)\delta(z). A simple unit quadrupole is \delta''(x)\delta(y)\delta(z) (pp. 91 to 92).

Dipole and quadrupole
\text{dipole}=-\delta'(x)\delta(y)\delta(z),\qquad \text{quadrupole}=\delta''(x)\delta(y)\delta(z)
4/5 · Multidimensional impulses

Use in physical equations

\delta'(x)\delta'(y) represents an electromagnetic radiator with a quatrefoil (clover-leaf) radiation pattern in the (x,y)-plane. All these symbolic expressions may confidently be inserted into Maxwell's equations, the wave equation, Poisson's equation and other fundamental differential equations (p. 92).

Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

Situation: The impulse symbol is convenient but we still want it on a rigorous footing.
Complication: The difficulty: \delta is not a function, and we also want to add them and differentiate them.
Resolution: Define a generalized function as a class of regular sequences of smooth functions, with operations defined by integration by parts.
  • Why: discontinuities, pure AC, finite resolution
  • Well-behaved functions and regular sequences
  • Algebra and derivatives of generalized functions
5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

Why we need generalized functions

The impulse symbol and combinations such as \text{III} and \mu bring much convenience, one use being to provide derivatives for functions with simple discontinuities. Ordinarily we would say the derivative does not exist; the impulse symbol permits such discontinuities to be accommodated (Bracewell, p. 92). The word "symbol" reminds us these are not functions.

5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

Pure alternating current: the infinitely remote past is convenient

Pure alternating current means an eternal harmonic variation, which cannot be generated. But the response to a variation that is harmonic over an interval and zero outside can be made independent of the switching time to a given precision by waiting long enough. Since the details of switching are irrelevant they may be relegated to the infinitely remote past (pp. 92 to 93). Likewise impulses cannot be generated, but responses to sufficiently brief pulses can be made indistinguishable at finite resolution.

5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

Finite resolution is the key

Precision of measurement is limited by the temporal and spectral resolution of the instrument. That feature is the key to the mathematical interpretation: integrals containing \delta are limits of a sequence of integrals in which \delta is replaced by unit-area rectangular pulses. The limit may exist even though the rectangular pulses grow without limit. The physical situation is a sequence of ever more compact stimuli producing responses that become indistinguishable however high the precision (p. 93).

5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

History of the approach

A satisfactory mathematical formulation was evolved along these lines and is expounded in the books of Lighthill (1958) and Friedman (1956). Lighthill credits Temple with simplifying the presentation; Temple (1953) credits the Polish mathematician Mikusiński (1948) with introducing the presentation in terms of sequences. Schwartz's two volumes (1950, 1951) on the theory of distributions unify partial and special techniques. The idea of sequences was current in physical circles before 1948, going back to G. S. Kirchhoff in 1882 (p. 93).

5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

The class S: particularly well-behaved functions

The class S consists of functions with derivatives of all orders at all points which, with all their derivatives, die off at least as rapidly as |x|^{-N} as |x|\to\infty, however large N. The derivative and the Fourier transform of a function in S are also in S (p. 94). The decay of the transform reflects smoothness: the transform of a Gaussian at s=3 is 5.3e-13, while for the rectangle |\text{sinc}(3.5)| = 0.0909, decaying only like 1/s.

5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

Regular sequences

Among sequences of particularly well-behaved functions we distinguish sequences p_\tau(x) which lead to limits when multiplied by any well-behaved F(x) and integrated: regular sequences. The sequence \tau^{-1}\exp(-\pi x^2/\tau^2) is regular; Fig. 5.15 shows a non-regular example. Unit area is not essential; for instance (1+\tau^{-1})\exp(-x^2/\tau^2) is also regular (pp. 94 to 95).

5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

A generalized function is a class of sequences

A generalized function p(x) is defined by a regular sequence. Since the limit can be the same for more than one sequence, it is finally the class of all equivalent regular sequences. The symbol p(x) stands for an entity different from an ordinary function: it stands for a class of functions and is itself not a function, so when written where ordinary functions are customary the meaning must be stated (p. 95). The sequences \tau^{-1}\Pi(x/\tau) and \tau^{-1}\Lambda(x/\tau) do not strictly define one since they lack derivatives of all orders, but they are usable where only low-order derivatives are needed (pp. 95 to 96).

5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

Proof that \tau^{-1}e^{-\pi x^2/\tau^2}\to\delta

The sequence e^{-\pi\tau^2x^2} defines the generalized function I(x)=1 since \lim\int e^{-\pi\tau^2x^2}F\,dx=\int F\,dx. The sequence \tau^{-1}e^{-\pi x^2/\tau^2} defines \delta: \bigl|\int\tau^{-1}e^{-\pi x^2/\tau^2}[F(x)-F(0)]dx\bigr|\le\max|F'|\int|x|\tau^{-1}e^{-\pi x^2/\tau^2}dx=\tau\max|F'|/\pi, which tends to 0 (p. 96). Measured with F=\cos x, \tau=0.1: the actual deviation 8.0e-04 is below the bound 3.2e-02.

Error bound
\left|\int\tau^{-1}e^{-\pi x^2/\tau^2}F(x)\,dx-F(0)\right|\ \le\ \frac{\tau}{\pi}\max|F'|
5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

Algebra: adding and differentiating generalized functions

The sum of two regular sequences is regular and independent of the choice of sequences, so p+q is meaningful (pp. 96 to 97). Derivatives are defined through integration by parts: \int p'F\,dx=-\int pF'\,dx, and in general \int p^{(n)}F\,dx=(-1)^n\int pF^{(n)}dx. Since well-behaved F has derivatives of every order, a generalized function has derivatives of every order (pp. 97 to 98).

Derivative of a generalized function
\int p'(x)\,F(x)\,dx=-\int p(x)\,F'(x)\,dx
5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

Differentiating ordinary functions: H'=\delta

An ordinary function of slow growth can also be regarded as a generalized function, so H(x) has a derivative: \int H'F\,dx=-\int H F'\,dx=-\int_0^\infty F'dx=F(0), while \int\delta F\,dx=F(0), hence H'=\delta (p. 98). Measured with F=e^{-x^2}: -\int_0^\infty F' = 1; with the derivative sequence of \tfrac12+\arctan(x/\tau)/\pi (a Lorentzian), \tau=0.1 gives 0.8965 and \tau=0.01 gives 0.9888: converging to 1 but slowly because of the Lorentzian tails.

5/5 · Generalized functions: the rigorous basis

Cross-reference to Barkat

Barkat, section 1.3.1 (Step and Impulse Functions), uses exactly these tools for random variables: the cumulative distribution of a discrete variable is a sum of steps \sum P_kH(x-x_k) and its density a sum of impulses \sum P_k\delta(x-x_k); mixed variables have both a continuous part and an impulsive part (section 1.3.4). Density of a discrete variable
f_X(x)=\sum_kP_k\,\delta(x-x_k)

SUMMARY

Takeaways

  • \delta is shorthand for a sequence of unit-area pulses; the pulse shape does not matter, only the integral.
  • Sifting: \int\delta f=f(0); scaling: \delta(ax)=\delta/|a|; x\delta=0 but a maximum \tfrac12 exists before the limit.
  • Shah samples under multiplication, replicates under convolution and is self-transforming; unit impulses spaced X are X^{-1}\text{III}(x/X).
  • \delta' is a dipole: \int\delta'f=-f'(0); null functions have zero integral and zero transform.
  • A generalized function is a class of regular sequences; derivatives are defined by integration by parts, so H'=\delta.

📜 Theory & historical background

The notation \delta(x) was first used by G. Kirchhoff, introduced into quantum mechanics by Dirac in 1927 and is now in general use. Historical examples of the impulse idea appear in Hermite, Cauchy, Poisson, Kirchhoff, Helmholtz, Kelvin and Heaviside (van der Pol and Bremmer, 1955) (Bracewell, p. 74).

The term "generalized function" was introduced by G. Temple in 1953; Dirac called \delta an "improper function"; Bracewell uses "symbol" systematically to mark entities that are not functions (p. 74). The rigorous theory is in Lighthill (1958) and Friedman (1956), Schwartz (1950, 1951) on distributions, and Mikusiński (1948) with the presentation through sequences (p. 93).

🔎 Real-world case study

Impulse response of a filter circuit. To characterise an RC circuit we do not need an ideal impulse, only a sufficiently brief pulse. With three differently shaped unit-area pulses (rectangle, triangle, two small pulses) the spread between the three responses at t=1 falls to 0.009 for width 0.5, 0.0003 for 0.1 and 0 for 0.01, i.e. the responses approach a single form h(t)=e^{-t}H(t). Engineers measure impulse responses by applying a pulse many times shorter than the circuit's time constant.

This is why defining \delta through sequences is reasonable: an instrument with finite resolution cannot distinguish a brief pulse from \delta.

⚠️ Common misreadings

"\delta(x) is a function equal to infinity at 0."

It is not a function: it is shorthand for a limit of integrals. The "infinite value" is meaningless; only the integral has meaning (pp. 74 to 75).

"\delta(ax)=\delta(x)."

Đúng là \delta(ax)=\delta(x)/|a|: the sifting integral for a=3 equals 0.3333, not 1 (p. 80).

"With spacing X, \text{III}(x/X) has unit impulses."

Wrong: the strength is X; unit impulses spaced X are X^{-1}\text{III}(x/X). Dropping the factor X^{-1} makes the sum of samples 2 instead of 1 (p. 83).

"x\delta(x)=0 so it vanishes completely."

Its integral is 0 but before the limit its maximum is 0.5 for every \tau (Gibbs-like); fed to an oscilloscope one would see spikes (p. 81).
