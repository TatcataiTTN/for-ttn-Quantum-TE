The integral Hartley transform

Situation: Hartley (1942) proposed a real transform with the kernel \mathrm{cas}\,\theta=\cos\theta+\sin\theta whose forward and inverse have the same form.
Complication: From its even and odd parts we recover the Fourier transform by reflection and addition; conversely take the real part minus the imaginary part.
Resolution: On the complex plane the Hartley transform is the component of the Fourier locus along an axis rotated by 45^\circ, so no information is lost.

1/5 · The integral Hartley transform

A strictly reciprocal real transform

Given a real waveform V(t) we define \psi(\omega)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int V(t)(\cos\omega t+\sin\omega t)\,dt. Clearly \psi is a sum of double-sided cosine and sine transforms, so the inverse has the same form: V(t)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int\psi(\omega)(\cos\omega t+\sin\omega t)\,d\omega. These relations were presented by Hartley in 1942 and never disappeared from the technical literature but have not been well known. Forward and inverse are identical, and if V(t) is real so is \psi(\omega) (Bracewell, p. 293). In this chapter Bracewell drops the factor (2\pi)^{-1/2} and moves to the familiar notation with 2\pi\times frequency.

The cas kernel
\operatorname{cas}\theta=\cos\theta+\sin\theta=\sqrt2\,\sin\!\left(\theta+\tfrac\pi4\right)
1/5 · The integral Hartley transform

From Hartley to Fourier: reflect and add

Write \psi(\omega)=e(\omega)+o(\omega) with e,o the even and odd parts: e(\omega)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int V\cos\omega t\,dt and o(\omega)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int V\sin\omega t\,dt. Then the Fourier transform S(\omega)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int V(\cos\omega t-i\sin\omega t)dt=e(\omega)-io(\omega) (3): from \psi we extract S by simple reflections and additions. Conversely \psi(\omega)=\mathrm{Re}\,[S(\omega)]-\mathrm{Im}\,[S(\omega)] (4): from S we get \psi as the sum of the real part and the sign-reversed imaginary part (pp. 293 to 294).

Hartley and Fourier
S=e-io,\qquad \psi=e+o=\operatorname{Re}S-\operatorname{Im}S
1/5 · The integral Hartley transform

Example: V(t)=e^{-t} for t>0

Dropping the factor (2\pi)^{-1/2}: the Fourier transform is S(\omega)=\dfrac{1-i\omega}{1+\omega^2} and the Hartley transform \psi(\omega)=\dfrac{1+\omega}{1+\omega^2} (Bracewell, p. 294, Fig. 12.1); \psi is real and clearly unsymmetrical. Numerical integration of \int_0^\infty e^{-t}\mathrm{cas}\,\omega t\,dt gives \psi(0) = 1, \psi(1) = 1, \psi(2) = 0.6 and \psi(-1) = 0. At \omega=1: \mathrm{Re}\,S = 0.5 and \mathrm{Im}\,S = -0.5, so \psi=\mathrm{Re}-\mathrm{Im} = 1. From the even and odd parts of \psi we recover S, as in the book's construction.

1/5 · The integral Hartley transform

Reciprocity checked numerically

With V(t)=e^{-\pi(t-0.5)^2} (an off-centre Gaussian, neither even nor odd) and kernel \mathrm{cas}\,2\pi st: the Fourier transform is e^{-\pi s^2}e^{-i\pi s}, so \psi(s)=e^{-\pi s^2}[\cos\pi s+\sin\pi s]. Applying the same formula a second time to \psi returns V: at t=0.3 we have V = 0.8819 and the reconstructed value deviates by 1e-16. No factor i or \pm pairs as in the Fourier case; this is what Hartley called more symmetrical.

1/5 · The integral Hartley transform

The view in the complex plane

Plot F(s)=R+iI as a locus in the complex plane (Fig. 2.10). Rotate the axes by -45^\circ: R'(s)+iI'(s)=[R(s)+iI(s)]e^{i\pi/4}. The component in the R' direction is the Hartley transform (times 1/\sqrt2), while the S' component adds no information if f is real. The locus can be reconstructed from R and I together; the absence of redundancy due to the symmetries of R and I permits the real f to be recovered from the R' direction alone, or from any direction except the R and S axes (p. 307). Measured at \omega=1 of the example: R' = 0.7071, and \psi/R' = 1.4142. For complex data the S' direction handles the remaining information and the Hartley transform becomes complex (Millane, 1994).

1/5 · The integral Hartley transform

Physical meaning: phase from amplitude

The radiation field of an antenna is the Fourier transform of the aperture distribution, and a lens converts an aperture distribution into another similarly expressible, so it might seem that Hartley is a mere mathematical construct. But an intimate relationship exists: whatever property one transform possesses, a corresponding property is possessed by the other. The first apparatus used an optical beamsplitter and Michelson interferometer technique (Bracewell et al., 1985); later microwave elements extended the wavelength range (Villasenor and Bracewell, 1988). In the Hartley plane phase is encoded by amplitude alone; of interest since at ultraviolet and x-ray wavelengths phase is important but difficult to measure (p. 307).

1/5 · The integral Hartley transform

Ralph Hartley and the history

Hartley was a Rhodes scholar, a Fellow of the Institute of Radio Engineers, in charge of telephone line research at the Bell Telephone Laboratories (1918 to 1929); to generations of electrical engineers his name was known through the Hartley oscillator, a textbook example of a sine-wave source with a single triode and feedback from a tapped inductance. The special issue of Proc. IEEE (March 1994, Olejniczak and Heydt) has hundreds of papers. When the fast Hartley algorithm appeared in 1984 there was some incredulity about the claimed speed, reality and reversibility; but hundreds of papers appeared, chips were manufactured and the physical significance was demonstrated in the laboratory with microwaves and visible light. It soon became clear that the relationship with Fourier amounts to full equivalency (Bracewell, pp. 296 to 297, 308 to 309).

The discrete Hartley transform

Situation: The DHT of N real numbers is N real numbers: H(\nu)=N^{-1}\sum f(\tau)\,\mathrm{cas}(2\pi\nu\tau/N).
Complication: The inverse has the same formula without the N^{-1}; the DFT is the even part minus i times the odd part of the DHT.
Resolution: No information is lost: N real numbers replace the 2N of the DFT because the Hermitian redundancy of the DFT is a factor of 2.

  • Bracewell's examples

2/5 · The discrete Hartley transform

The DHT definition

For a discrete variable \tau=0,\ldots,N-1 and real f(\tau), H(\nu)=N^{-1}\sum_{\tau=0}^{N-1}f(\tau)\,\mathrm{cas}(2\pi\nu\tau/N), where \mathrm{cas}\,\theta=\cos\theta+\sin\theta. Compare the DFT F(\nu)=N^{-1}\sum f(\tau)e^{-i2\pi\nu\tau/N}. The inverse: f(\tau)=\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}H(\nu)\,\mathrm{cas}(2\pi\nu\tau/N) (Bracewell, p. 295). The proof uses the orthogonality \sum_{\nu}\mathrm{cas}(2\pi\nu\tau/N)\,\mathrm{cas}(2\pi\nu\tau'/N)=N if \tau=\tau' and 0 otherwise. Measured with N=16: the deviation of the orthogonality sum is 5e-14, and transforming and inverting random data returns the data with deviation 6e-15.

DHT and inverse
H(\nu)=\frac1N\sum_{\tau}f(\tau)\operatorname{cas}\frac{2\pi\nu\tau}N,\qquad f(\tau)=\sum_{\nu}H(\nu)\operatorname{cas}\frac{2\pi\nu\tau}N
2/5 · The discrete Hartley transform

Quasi-symmetry and the strictly symmetric version

The DHT is quasi-symmetrical: forward and inverse differ only by the factor N^{-1} familiar from the DFT, and it is real. To make it strictly symmetrical, N^{-1} can be changed to N^{-1/2} in both directions, as Hartley did with his integrals. But in computing it is often convenient to stop short of multiplying by the constant, letting it be absorbed later into a normalisation or graphics scale factor. With N^{-1/2} the matrix \mathbf C_{\nu\tau}=N^{-1/2}\mathrm{cas}(2\pi\nu\tau/N) is symmetric and \mathbf C^2=\mathbf I: measured N=8, \|\mathbf C^2-\mathbf I\| = 2e-15. So \mathbf C is an involution and all its eigenvalues are \pm1 (p. 296).

2/5 · The discrete Hartley transform

From the DHT to the DFT and back

Split H(\nu)=E(\nu)+O(\nu) with even part E(\nu)=\tfrac12[H(\nu)+H(N-\nu)] and odd part O(\nu)=\tfrac12[H(\nu)-H(N-\nu)]. Then F(\nu)=E(\nu)-iO(\nu); conversely H(\nu)=\mathrm{Re}\,F(\nu)-\mathrm{Im}\,F(\nu) (p. 296). The power spectrum and Hermitian relations can accordingly be read directly from H. Measured with random data N=16: F=E-iO deviates from the FFT by 8e-16, and H=\mathrm{Re}\,F-\mathrm{Im}\,F deviates by 1e-15. For a DHT from existing software: run the complex FFT and subtract the imaginary parts from the real parts, using only half of the values (p. 324).

2/5 · The discrete Hartley transform

Short pairs: N=2, N=4, N=8

N=2: \{a\ b\} has DHT \tfrac12\{a+b\ \ a-b\}; for \{1,2\} we get 1.5 and -0.5 (p. 309). N=4: \{1,2,3,4\} has DHT \{2.5,-1,-0.5,0\} (p. 310). N=8: \{1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8\} has DHT \{4.5,\,{-1.7071},\,{-1},\,{-0.7071},\,{-0.5},\,{-0.2929},\,0,\,0.7071\} (p. 314; the book prints rounded values): the measurements are H(1) = -1.7071, H(3) = -0.7071, H(5) = -0.2929, H(7) = 0.7071, and H(6) = 0. Check: the sum of the data equals N\times H(0) = 36, and the sum of the DHT values equals f(0) = 1.

2/5 · The discrete Hartley transform

A 16-point example: the truncated exponential

Take f(0)=0.5 (the mean of the two values at the discontinuity of V(t)) and f(\tau)=e^{-\tau/2} for 1\le\tau\le15: a representation of the continuous function e^{-t/2}H(t) by N=16 samples (Fig. 12.2). The DHT closely resembles samples of the continuous transform taken at intervals 1/16; the small discrepancies are due to truncation and aliasing, exactly as with the DFT. Measured: H(0) = 0.1275, H(1) = 0.1386 against the continuous value \psi(1/16)/16 = 0.138, with \psi(s)=(0.5+2\pi s)/(0.25+4\pi^2s^2). This is the Hartley version of the DFT-versus-FT problem of module 14.

2/5 · The discrete Hartley transform

The binomial example: a smooth hump

The binomial sequence 1,6,15,20,15,6,1 represents samples of a smooth pulse; to obtain the simplest result the peak is placed at \tau=0: f=\{20,15,6,1,0,\ldots,0,1,6,15\} (N=16, even about 0). The DHT therefore equals the real DFT: H(\nu)=\{4,\ 3.56,\ 2.49,\ 1.32,\ 0.5,\ 0.12,\ 0.01,\ 0,\ \ldots\} and symmetric H(N-\nu)=H(\nu): a smooth pulse peaking at \nu=0 (Fig. 12.3). The measurements: H(1) = 3.56, H(2) = 2.49, H(4) = 0.5. (The copy of the book used here prints a 0 at \nu=4, whereas the symmetry H(12)=0.5 requires 0.5; this may be a scanning or printing error.)

2/5 · The discrete Hartley transform

Why N reals can replace 2N reals

At first sight it seems strange: the DFT has N complex numbers, 2N real numbers in all. But the Hermitian property means redundancy by a factor of 2: N/2 real numbers suffice for the cosine transform and N/2 for the sine transform, together forming N DHT coefficients with no degeneracy due to symmetry. The cas function is a sine wave shifted 45^\circ, so it responds equally to cosine and sine components. With a kernel \sqrt2\sin(\theta+\alpha) with arbitrary \alpha the responses are unequal but no information is lost unless \alpha=0,\pi/2,\ldots; the inverse uses a combination of \sin\theta and \cos\theta with \alpha-dependent weights (Bracewell, p. 298; the weights 1/(\sqrt2\sin\alpha) and 1/(\sqrt2\cos\alpha) below are our result). With \alpha=0.6: the data are recovered with deviation 6e-15.

Theorems and convolution by the DHT

Situation: There is a Hartley theorem for every Fourier theorem; some match exactly, others (shift, convolution) differ.
Complication: Convolution with the DHT needs two real multiplications per element instead of the four of the DFT, and only one when a function is even.
Resolution: The two-dimensional DHT is a real matrix, and the power spectrum reads directly from H(\nu) and H(N-\nu).

  • The convolution theorem
  • Two dimensions, cas-cas, power spectrum
3/5 · Theorems and convolution by the DHT

Theorems that match exactly

There is a Hartley theorem for every Fourier theorem; some match exactly: \sum H(\nu)=f(0) and \sum f(\tau)=NH(0). Reversal: f(-\tau) has DHT H(-\nu), with arguments outside 0\ldots N-1 interpreted modulo N. Quadratic content (Parseval): \sum|f(\tau)|^2=N\sum|H(\nu)|^2, like the DFT but with no complex conjugates, only real numbers (Bracewell, pp. 300 to 301). Measured for \{1,\ldots,8\}: \sum f^2 = 204 and N\sum H^2 = 204; the reversal theorem deviates by 1e-15 for random data. Cross-check with the DFT: \sum|F|^2=\tfrac12\sum[H(\nu)^2+H(N-\nu)^2] coincides with \sum H^2.

3/5 · Theorems and convolution by the DHT

The shift and first-difference theorems

Unlike Fourier, the shift reads: the DHT of f(\tau+a) equals H(\nu)\cos(2\pi\nu a/N)-H(-\nu)\sin(2\pi\nu a/N) (p. 300), needed for the fast Hartley algorithm. The first-difference theorem: f(\tau+1)-f(\tau) has DHT [\cos(2\pi\nu/N)-1]H(\nu)-\sin(2\pi\nu/N)H(N-\nu) (p. 301). Measured for random N=16, a=3: the shift theorem deviates by 2e-15, and the difference theorem by 1e-15. Mind the sign: swapping the minus for a plus gives an error of about 0.7, so the theorem must be written with the right sign.

3/5 · Theorems and convolution by the DHT

The general convolution theorem

If f=f_1*f_2 is the cyclic convolution then H(\nu)=H_1(\nu)H_{2e}(\nu)+H_1(-\nu)H_{2o}(\nu), where H_2=H_{2e}+H_{2o} are the even and odd parts (p. 299; as in the DFT convolution theorem f_1*f_2\supset NF_1F_2 there is also a factor N when H contains N^{-1}). Measured with two random N=16 sequences: the relation holds with the factor N, deviation 1e-14. If f_2 is even then H_{2o}=0 and H=NH_1H_2: one real product per element. Convolution by the DFT needs one complex multiplication per element, i.e. 4 real multiplications; by the DHT only 2 in the general case.

3/5 · Theorems and convolution by the DHT

The convolution procedure with the DHT

In image processing and digital filtering one function is often even (a symmetric filter). Then f_1*f_2=\mathrm{DHT}[(\mathrm{DHT}\,f_1)\times(\mathrm{DHT}\,f_2)]: take two DHTs, multiply the two real sequences term by term, and take one more DHT (with a factor N depending on the normalisation convention). Beforehand extend both sequences to double length with zeros to make room for the result; the zeros may lead, trail or bracket the data. If one sequence is much shorter, as in ordinary digital filtering, direct convolution is recommended. With \{1,2,3,4\} and the even filter \{0.25,0.5,0.25\} padded to 16: the result matches direct convolution with deviation 4e-15; the result \{0.25,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 3.25,\ 1\} has largest element 3.

3/5 · Theorems and convolution by the DHT

The two-dimensional DHT

An image f(\tau_1,\tau_2) as an M\times N matrix has the DHT as a real matrix H(\nu_1,\nu_2)=M^{-1}N^{-1}\sum\sum f\,\mathrm{cas}[2\pi(\nu_1\tau_1/M+\nu_2\tau_2/N)] and the inverse has the same formula without the factor (p. 299). It can be broken into N one-dimensional transforms. Benefit: the full spectral analysis of an image can be presented in a single diagram (Fourier needs two: real and imaginary parts, or amplitude and phase); one real array is stored. The two-dimensional power spectrum is the same for both transforms. Measured with a 6\times5 image: H=\mathrm{Re}\,F-\mathrm{Im}\,F with F the 2D DFT, deviation 6e-16; and transforming twice returns the image with deviation 4e-15. Three dimensions: Hao and Bracewell (1987); four: Buneman (1987).

3/5 · Theorems and convolution by the DHT

The cas-cas transform

In two dimensions one may use the kernel \mathrm{cas}\,2\pi\nu_1\tau_1\,\mathrm{cas}\,2\pi\nu_2\tau_2, which is separable (Perkins, 1987). The cas-cas transform is also reciprocal (Bracewell, 1983) and is related to the standard DHT the way choosing between Fourier components \cos2\pi(\nu_1\tau_1+\nu_2\tau_2), corrugations with parallel null loci, and \cos\cos with null loci at right angles. Separability has not proved a basis for preference, so the kernel \mathrm{cas}[2\pi(\nu_1\tau_1+\nu_2\tau_2)] became the standard (p. 300). Measured on an 8\times8 image: the cas-cas transform applied twice returns the image (deviation 6e-15) but differs from the standard DHT: largest difference 1.52.

3/5 · Theorems and convolution by the DHT

The power spectrum directly from the DHT

If the power spectrum |F(\nu)|^2=R^2+X^2 is wanted one need not go through complex numbers: |F(\nu)|^2=\tfrac12[H(\nu)^2+H(N-\nu)^2] (p. 314). For \{8,7,6,5,4,3,2,1\} (Problem 4): H=\{4.5,\ 1.7071,\ 1,\ 0.7071,\ 0.5,\ 0.2929,\ 0,\ -0.7071\}, the even part E(1) = 0.5, the odd part O(1) = 1.2071, so F(1)=E-iO = 0.5 - 1.2071i and |F(1)|^2 = 1.7071, matching the direct formula (2e-15). Deviation over all \nu for random data: 3e-16.

Situation: Evaluating the DHT directly takes N^2 operations; for N=2^P they can be reduced to about N\log_2N by successive halving.
Complication: The decomposition formula H(\nu)=H_1+H_2\cos+H_2(N-\nu)\sin follows from the shift and similarity theorems.
Resolution: Bit-reversal permutation takes a share of the time that does not die out, so optimising it pays.

  • The N=8 hand example

4/5 · The fast Hartley transform

Motivation and merits

A sequence of N real numbers has a DHT that is a sequence of the same length and is also real; applying the same formula a second time returns the original sequence. No need to manage real and imaginary parts in two arrays, or interleaved in one array of double length, and no inverse that differs from the forward. If the real and imaginary parts of the DFT are wanted they come from the even and odd parts of the DHT; the power spectrum directly. One-way FFT programs for real data skip the conjugate coefficients but the output is complex, so a separate inverse program must be stored, and neither can accept its own output. Bracewell states the FHT is faster than the complex FFT by a factor depending on circumstances (p. 308).

4/5 · The fast Hartley transform

The even/odd decomposition formula

For N=2: \{a\ b\} has DHT \tfrac12\{a+b\ \ a-b\}. Split f into the even-indexed sequence (DHT H_1) and the odd-indexed one (DHT H_2), each of length N/2. Using the similarity theorem (stretching: the DHT repeats) for the even part and the shift theorem with a=-1 for the odd part: H(\nu)=\tfrac12\left[H_1(\nu)+H_2(\nu)\cos\tfrac{2\pi\nu}N+H_2(N-\nu)\sin\tfrac{2\pi\nu}N\right] with H_1,H_2 understood periodic with period N/2; in the text the factors \tfrac12 are postponed to the end (pp. 309 to 310). Measured: for random N=16 the formula matches the direct DHT, deviation 4e-15. In the first stages after permutation \cos,\sin take only the values 0, 1, -1 so only additions and subtractions remain.

The Hartley decomposition
H(\nu)=\tfrac12\Big[H_1(\nu)+H_2(\nu)\cos\tfrac{2\pi\nu}{N}+H_2(N-\nu)\sin\tfrac{2\pi\nu}{N}\Big]
4/5 · The fast Hartley transform

Bit-reversal permutation

Permutation rearranges the sequence to bisect progressively down to pairs: for 8 elements the order is f(0),f(4),f(2),f(6),f(1),f(5),f(3),f(7). Write the index i in binary (000 to 111), reverse the digits and reconvert to decimal to give the new index j. Half of the indices (0, 2, 5, 7) remain unchanged because their binary representations are symmetrical, so skipping them saves time; the permutation reduces to two swaps 1\leftrightarrow4 and 3\leftrightarrow6. Scatter diagrams of j versus i break down into replicated cells (the kite for N=4, the frog for N=8) (pp. 321 to 323). Measured: the number of fixed indices is 4 for N=8 and 32 for N=1024 (2^{\lceil P/2\rceil}); for N=8 there are 2 swapped pairs.

4/5 · The fast Hartley transform

Counting multiplications: FHT against FFT

Implement the decomposition formula recursively and count the multiplications by factors other than 0,\pm1. For N=1024 the FHT uses 14344 real multiplications; the halving FFT has 3586 genuine complex multiplications, i.e. 14344 real multiplications (four real per complex), equal in this raw form: the advantage of the FHT is that it computes N real numbers (half the storage and output) instead of N complex numbers, and real input does not need the redundant Hermitian half of the spectrum. Bracewell reports by experiment on a personal computer: the figure of merit Y ranges from 3.1 to 5.8 for a translated FFT program and about 1.3 when the FHT is used to obtain the Fourier transform (p. 315): running-time numbers on one machine, not reproducible here.

4/5 · The fast Hartley transform

The stripe diagram: time by component

Bracewell analyses running time against N in the stripe diagram (Fig. 12.5). Three kinds of component: fixed overhead that dies out when normalised by NP; precalculation of sines and cosines, proportional to N, so its stripe thins like P^{-1}; and permutation, N reassignments repeated P times, proportional to NP, so its stripe does not die out: any effort to streamline permutation is worthwhile (like replacing J/2 by 0.5\times J). The nondecaying zone has thickness 0.038 s on the author's computer, so the limiting time is 0.038NP; near P\approx10 it is about one third higher. Stages 1 and 2 use the short equations of Table 12.2 (no trigonometry) for speed (pp. 315 to 317). The radix-4 algorithm saves about 25 percent of the running time, for N a power of 4 (16, 64, 256, ...) (p. 323).

4/5 · The fast Hartley transform

The matrix representation and the DFT factorisation

One can write \mathbf H=\mathbf L_P\cdots\mathbf L_2\mathbf L_1\,\mathbf P\,\mathbf f (with \mathbf P the permutation matrix and \mathbf L_i sparse matrix operators of level i), and proceed to the DFT \mathbf F=\mathbf W\mathbf f by multiplying with a (complex) conversion matrix; combined this gives a new factorisation of the DFT matrix \mathbf W with W=e^{-i2\pi/N} (pp. 317 to 320). \mathbf L_1 and \mathbf L_2 have only 2N nonzero elements compared with 3N for the higher levels; \mathbf L_1 has sine elements on lines of slope 45^\circ. Measured: the 16\times16 cas matrix has 224 nonzero elements out of 256 (the zeros are exactly where \mathrm{cas}=0).

5/5 · Discrete sine and cosine transforms and data compression

The DFT-type cosine transform is not invertible

The discrete cosine transform \sum f(\tau)\cos(2\pi\nu\tau/N) (and likewise the sine) cannot be inverted to recover f(\tau) because components of the form \sin(2\pi\nu\tau/N) are integrated out and leave no trace. They are invertible when attention is restricted to even f (f(\tau)=f(N-\tau) for \tau=1\ldots N-1), and the integral cosine and sine transforms are invertible if f(x) is zero for negative x (Bracewell, pp. 301 to 302). Measured: the sequence \{1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8\} and its reversal give the same cosine transform (difference 2e-14) though they are different sequences (data difference 6).

5/5 · Discrete sine and cosine transforms and data compression

The invertible DST and DCT-1 with half-integer frequencies

Change the frequencies to 0,\tfrac1{2N},\tfrac1N,\tfrac3{2N},\ldots: the fundamental frequency is 1/2N and the fundamental period 2N. The DST: F_s(\nu)=\tfrac2N\sum_{\tau=1}^{N-1}f(\tau)\sin(\pi\nu\tau/N), inverse f(\tau)=\sum_{\nu=1}^{N-1}F_s(\nu)\sin(\pi\nu\tau/N); applying the sum \sum f\sin twice returns \tfrac12Nf(\tau). The value f(0) leaves no trace since \sin is zero there. DCT-1 (Rao and Yip, 1990) is strictly symmetric with N+1 values: F(\nu)=\sqrt{2/N}\,k_\nu\sum_{\tau=0}^Nk_\tau f(\tau)\cos(\pi\nu\tau/N), k=1/\sqrt2 at both ends and 1 elsewhere (pp. 301 to 302). Measured N=16: the DST inverse deviates by 3e-15; applying it twice gives the factor 8; DCT-1 applied twice deviates by 2e-15.

5/5 · Discrete sine and cosine transforms and data compression

Boundary-value problems: the string

A violin string of length L drawn aside at a noncentral point: the boundary conditions y(0)=y(L)=0 render cosine terms unnecessary so only \sin(\pi x/L),\sin(2\pi x/L),\ldots are needed to synthesise the string shape. If instead the condition is [dy/dx]_{x=0}=0 cosine components alone may suffice. It can be done with the full Fourier transform but is not customary since it yields both kinds (Bracewell, pp. 302 to 303). Our example (a string plucked at a=0.3, L=1): y(x)=x/a for x<a and (1-x)/(1-a) for x>a, sine coefficients b_n=\dfrac{2\sin n\pi a}{n^2\pi^2a(1-a)}: b_1 = 0.7807, b_2 = 0.2294; a 300-term sum gives y(0.5) = 0.7143 against the exact 0.7143, deviation 8e-08. Waves in a canal with closed ends analyse into cosines alone.

Differential equations and dimensions

Fields of physics involving the wave equation, Laplace's equation, the diffusion equation and other fundamental differential equations in two, three and four dimensions provide a wide range of application for numerical analysis using the discrete sine and cosine transforms (p. 303). The reason: natural boundary conditions choose the basis functions, and if the sine functions are solutions of the eigenproblem the transform turns differential operators into multiplication (modules 13 and 19: the eigensolutions of \phi''+\lambda\phi=0 are \sin k\pi t). A numerical illustration: the 50\times50 second-difference matrix has eigenvalues 2-2\cos(k\pi/51) and eigenvectors \sin(k\pi j/51): the deviation between the numerical eigenvalues and the formula is 2e-15.

Data compression and transform encoding

An image of a million samples each with 256 levels: a fourfold reduction in bits results if 2\times2 pixel blocks are replaced by one mean pixel, but the image blurs, describable as convolution followed by coarser sampling. Empirically many images have little content at high spatial frequencies, suggesting transmitting the Fourier transform of the image with the higher frequencies suppressed or deemphasised (for example quantised to fewer levels), and inverting at the receiver: called transform encoding. Further gain comes from one-dimensional encoding (run-length, TIFF, delta-row) and segmenting the image into parts of uniform statistics; text, ASCII and digitised speech benefit little (Bracewell, pp. 303 to 304). Measured: 2\times2 blocks reduce the bits by a factor 4; and a further reduction of 32 since newsprint offers only about 8 grey levels: 256/8 = 32.

5/5 · Discrete sine and cosine transforms and data compression

DCT-2: the transform of image compression

Ahmed, Natarajan and Rao (1974) introduced {}^{\mathrm{II}}F(\nu)=\sum f(\tau)\cos[\pi\nu(2\tau+1)/2N] (normalised by \sqrt{2/N}\,a(\nu) with a(0)=1/\sqrt2), and it entered an international standard (JPEG). DCT-2 analyses into frequencies 0,\tfrac1{2N},\tfrac1N,\ldots like DCT-1 but the samples are shifted by half a unit, so the basis functions are no longer purely cosinusoidal; the basis vectors remain orthogonal. Bracewell stresses that this transform is not strictly invertible (applying it twice does not return the object): measured \|\mathbf C^2-\mathbf I\| = 1.99 but \mathbf C\mathbf C^T=\mathbf I deviates by 2e-15. The shift and convolution theorems become opaque. The claimed advantage: empirical performance on images whose statistics approximate spatially uniform first-order Markov statistics (p. 304).

Why DCT-2 suits first-order Markov data: energy compaction

A first-order Markov process has autocorrelation r(k)=\rho^{|k|} (module 14, AR(1)). With \rho=0.95 and block N=16, sort the coefficient variances (the diagonal of \mathbf TR\mathbf T^T) in decreasing order and keep the four largest: the fraction of energy retained is 0.9557 for DCT-2, 0.9364 for the normalised DHT, 0.9559 for the Karhunen-Loève transform (the optimum bound, module 13), and only 0.25 with no transform (4 samples). So DCT-2 nearly reaches the optimum bound; this is why it is empirically preferred for compression (a numerical test of the remark on p. 304; the exact values are our result).

A feel for indices: cosine, sine and dc in the DHT

Bracewell recalls that a frequency f is represented at two locations \nu=\pm Nf mod N; cosine components have equal values, sine components equal values with opposite signs (pp. 305 to 306). With N=8: \{1,0,-1,0,1,0,-1,0\} (\cos2\pi\tau/4) has DHT \tfrac12 at \nu=2 and 6: 0.5 and 0.5; \{0,1,0,-1,0,1,0,-1\} (\sin) has DHT 0.5 at \nu=2 and -0.5 at \nu=6; adding dc 2: \{3,2,1,2,3,2,1,2\} has DHT 2 at \nu=0. With the other transforms, DCT-1 and DCT-2 of these very sequences give different numbers because they are not Fourier: the frequency meaning of a DCT spectrum must be read with care (p. 306).

SUMMARY

Takeaways

  • Hartley: \psi=\mathrm{Re}\,S-\mathrm{Im}\,S with kernel \mathrm{cas}=\cos+\sin; forward and inverse have the same form; it is the component of the Fourier locus along a 45^\circ axis.
  • DHT: N real numbers in, N real numbers out; F=E-iO, H=\mathrm{Re}F-\mathrm{Im}F; \{1,2,3,4\}\to\{2.5,-1,-0.5,0\}.
  • Theorems: shift H\cos-H(-\nu)\sin; Parseval \sum f^2=N\sum H^2; convolution H_1H_{2e}+H_1(-\nu)H_{2o}; power spectrum \tfrac12[H(\nu)^2+H(N-\nu)^2].
  • FHT: bit-reversal permutation then the even/odd decomposition H=\tfrac12[H_1+H_2\cos+H_2(N-\nu)\sin]; the raw multiplication count equals the FFT but with real numbers only.
  • DST and DCT-1 are invertible (half-integer frequencies); DCT-2 (JPEG) is orthogonal but not an involution and packs the energy of a first-order Markov process almost like Karhunen-Loève.

📜 Theory & historical background

Hartley presented the transform in 1942 (Proc. IRE 30, pp. 144 to 150); Bracewell introduced the DHT (1983) and the fast Hartley transform (1984), and a monograph in 1986; Buneman (1986) linked the FFT to the FHT; the special issue of Proc. IEEE in March 1994. DCT-2 is due to Ahmed, Natarajan and Rao (1974), later entering the JPEG standard (Pennebaker and Mitchell, 1993) (Bracewell, pp. 293 to 325).

🔎 Real-world case study

Fast convolution of real data. Filtering an image with a symmetric filter: the convolution is done with two DHTs, one real term-by-term product and one DHT (instead of four real multiplications per element for the DFT), with a result matching direct convolution (4e-15), and the power spectrum read straight from H. Compressing 16-sample blocks of a Markov signal with \rho=0.95: four DCT-2 coefficients already keep 0.9557 of the energy, close to the Karhunen-Loève bound 0.9559, against 0.25 if only four samples are kept; on top of a fourfold reduction by 2\times2 merging and 32-fold by quantisation. The zero at \nu=3N/4 of \{1,\ldots,N\} is a peculiarity of the DHT.

⚠️ Common misreadings

"The Hartley transform differs from Fourier so it loses phase."

Nothing is lost: F=E-iO so amplitude and phase are recoverable; for example |F(1)|^2 of \{8,\ldots,1\} = 1.7071.

"The DHT shift theorem is identical to the DFT one, just multiply by \cos."

There is also the term -H(-\nu)\sin; swapping the sign gives an error of 0.7 (the correct one deviates 2e-15).

"DCT-2 is its own inverse like the DHT."

No: \|\mathbf C^2-\mathbf I\| = 1.99, whereas the DHT involution deviates 2e-15.

"The FHT always needs fewer multiplications than the FFT."

Raw count for N=1024: 14344 real (FHT) and 14344 real (FFT); the gain lies in storage and real arithmetic, not in the count.
