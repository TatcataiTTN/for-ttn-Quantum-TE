The signal-processing workflow, step by step

Twelve steps from posing the problem to reporting. Each has a theory frame, frequently asked questions, and a worked example with numbers computed for real, plus links to the related modules.

1Pose the problem and the signal modelM1M9

🎓 Theory

Before computing, decide whether the signal is deterministic or random, continuous or discrete, and whether the system is linear and invariant. If it is, the response is a convolution and the frequency response describes the system completely (Bracewell, chapters 1 and 9).

Response of a linear invariant system
y=h*x\;\Longleftrightarrow\;Y(s)=H(s)\,X(s)

❓ Frequently asked

Why check linearity and invariance first?Because every later Fourier tool relies on both conditions. Module 1 shows that a squarer and a cosine multiplier create new frequencies.
Does a random signal have a spectrum?It has a power spectrum (module 12), not an ordinary Fourier transform for each realisation.

📌 Worked example with real numbers

Feed a 50 Hz sinusoid into an amplifier. If the output has an extra 100 Hz line, the system is nonlinear (module 1).

2Sampling and anti-aliasingM13

🎓 Theory

To keep a signal you must sample at f_s above twice its highest frequency. A component above f_s/2 is folded onto a lower frequency and cannot be separated after sampling (Bracewell, chapter 10; Barkat, section 3.8).

Aliased frequency
f_{\text{alias}}=\left|f-\operatorname{round}\!\left(\tfrac{f}{f_s}\right)f_s\right|
  • f_ssampling rate

❓ Frequently asked

Why filter before sampling?Because after sampling the true and the folded frequency look identical.
Does oversampling hurt?It does not make the result wrong; it only costs storage and computation.

📌 Worked example with real numbers

A 5000 Hz wave sampled at f_s=8000 Hz (Nyquist 4000 Hz). The FFT peaks at 3000 Hz, exactly |5000-8000|.

3Observation window and frequency resolutionM1M8M14

🎓 Theory

Observing for time T resolves only lines at least 1/T apart. Cutting the signal with a rectangular window leaks the spectrum into neighbouring bins (module 1); this is the convolution with a sinc function seen from the other side.

Resolution
\Delta f=\frac{f_s}{N}=\frac1T

❓ Frequently asked

Does zero-padding raise the resolution?No. It only interpolates a smoother curve; the resolution is still set by T.
What does a Hann window do?It reduces leakage but widens the lines, trading away resolution.

📌 Worked example with real numbers

With f_s=8000 Hz and 1024 samples the bin spacing is 7.8125 Hz. To separate two lines 5 Hz apart you need T\ge0.2 s, i.e. at least 1600 samples.

4Transform and read the spectrumM2M3M4M5M6M7M14

🎓 Theory

Pick a convention (where the 2\pi sits) and exploit symmetry: a real signal has a Hermitian spectrum, so half of it suffices. The shift, similarity, derivative and convolution theorems (chapter 6) find transforms without integrating (chapter 7).

Hermitian symmetry
f(x)\ \text{real}\;\Longrightarrow\;F(-s)=F^{*}(s)

❓ Frequently asked

What symmetry does a real signal have in frequency?Hermitian: F(-s)=F^*(s), so the magnitude is even and the phase odd (module 2).
Why does phase matter?Phase carries position: shifting the signal only multiplies the spectrum by a phase factor (module 6).

📌 Worked example with real numbers

A real 1000-sample signal has 1000 complex FFT coefficients, but thanks to the Hermitian property only 501 are needed (rfft).

5Linear filteringM3M9

🎓 Theory

A filter is a convolution with the impulse response and, in the frequency domain, a multiplication by the frequency response. An FIR filter (running mean) is always stable; a recursive one such as y[n]=a\,y[n-1]+(1-a)x[n] is cheaper but stability must be checked (modules 1, 3 and 9).

Magnitude response of an L-point running mean
|H(f)|=\left|\frac{\sin(\pi L f/f_s)}{L\,\sin(\pi f/f_s)}\right|

❓ Frequently asked

How does FIR differ from IIR?FIR uses a finite number of input samples and is always stable; IIR has feedback, so it is compact but can become unstable.
Does a filter change frequencies?Not if it is linear and invariant: it only changes the amplitude and phase of each component (module 1).

📌 Worked example with real numbers

An 8-point running mean at f_s=8000 Hz has gain 0.640729 at 500 Hz and 0 at 1000 Hz (a null). Both freqz and the closed form agree.

6Noise and random-variable modelsM10M11

🎓 Theory

The density of a sum of independent random variables is the convolution of the densities, i.e. the characteristic functions multiply. The same convolution, the same theorem (Bracewell, chapter 16; Barkat, chapters 1 and 2).

Density of a sum
f_{X+Y}=f_X*f_Y\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\varphi_{X+Y}=\varphi_X\,\varphi_Y

❓ Frequently asked

Why is a sum of many variables often Gaussian?That is the central limit theorem (Bracewell, chapter 8, section Central Limit Theorem; Barkat, section 1.4.3 covers the law of large numbers).
Where does the Rayleigh distribution appear?In the envelope of bandpass noise (Bracewell, chapter 17; Barkat, section 2.3.6).

📌 Worked example with real numbers

The sum of two independent variables uniform on [-\tfrac12,\tfrac12] has a triangular density. Numerical convolution gives variance 0.16667, matching 1/12+1/12=1/6.

7Power-spectrum estimationM12M20

🎓 Theory

The power spectrum is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation. The periodogram of one record has a standard deviation about equal to its own value and does not shrink as N grows, so several records must be averaged (Bracewell, chapters 17 and 19; Barkat, sections 3.5 and 3.7).

Wiener–Khinchin theorem
S(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(\tau)\,e^{-i2\pi s\tau}\,d\tau
  • R(\tau)autocorrelation function
  • S(s)power spectrum

❓ Frequently asked

Is the periodogram consistent?No: the variance does not fall as samples increase; segment averaging (Welch style) is needed.
What is ergodicity for?So a time average over one realisation can stand in for the ensemble average (Barkat, section 3.7).

📌 Worked example with real numbers

Unit white noise, 1024 samples per record: one periodogram has standard deviation 1.02 (theory 1), averaging 64 records leaves 0.125 (theory 0.125).

8Detecting a signal in noiseM21M24M25

🎓 Theory

Detection chooses between H_0 (noise only) and H_1 (signal present). The Neyman–Pearson criterion fixes the false-alarm probability P_{FA} and maximises the detection probability P_D; the solution is a likelihood-ratio test (Barkat, chapters 5 and 10).

Detection probability
P_D=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-d\right),\qquad d=\frac{\sqrt N\,A}{\sigma}
  • Qupper-tail function of the normal distribution

❓ Frequently asked

Why Neyman–Pearson rather than Bayes?Because it needs neither prior probabilities nor decision costs, which radar and communications often lack.
How do more samples help?The detectability index d=\sqrt N A/\sigma grows with \sqrt N, so P_D rises at the same P_{FA}.

📌 Worked example with real numbers

Detecting a DC level A=1 in Gaussian noise \sigma=1 with N=10 samples and P_{FA}=10^{-3}: the threshold on the sample mean is 0.9772, d=3.1623, P_D=0.5287 by formula and 0.528 by a 400 000-trial simulation.

9Parameter estimationM22

🎓 Theory

Maximum-likelihood (ML) estimation picks the parameter that makes the observed data most likely; Bayes estimation adds a prior (MAP, MMSE). The Cramér–Rao bound gives the smallest variance of any unbiased estimator (Barkat, chapter 6).

Cramér–Rao bound for a DC level
\operatorname{var}(\hat A)\ \ge\ \frac{\sigma^2}{N}

❓ Frequently asked

What is an estimator that reaches the Cramér–Rao bound called?An efficient estimator.
How does MAP differ from ML?MAP multiplies in the prior density of the parameter; with a flat prior it coincides with ML.

📌 Worked example with real numbers

Estimating a DC level from N=10 samples of noise \sigma=1: the Cramér–Rao bound is 0.1, the simulated variance of the sample mean is 0.1001, so the sample mean is an efficient estimator.

10Optimal filtering: Wiener and KalmanM23

🎓 Theory

The orthogonality principle says the error of a least-squares estimate must be orthogonal to the data. The Wiener filter applies it to stationary processes; the Kalman filter works recursively for time-varying systems (Barkat, sections 7.2, 7.3 and 7.5).

Scalar Wiener gain
w=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma_n^2},\qquad \text{MSE}=\frac{\sigma_s^2\sigma_n^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma_n^2}

❓ Frequently asked

How do Wiener and Kalman differ?Wiener is for stationary processes and designed in frequency; Kalman updates recursively sample by sample.
Does a Wiener filter always reduce the error?It gives the smallest mean-square error among linear filters, so it is never worse than leaving the data alone.

📌 Worked example with real numbers

A scalar signal of variance 1 in noise of variance 0.25: Wiener gain 0.8, mean-square error 0.2 (simulated 0.2006) versus 0.2504 if the data is left alone.

11Adaptive CFAR thresholdsM26

🎓 Theory

When the noise power varies, a fixed threshold lets the false-alarm probability drift. CFAR estimates the noise level from reference cells around the cell under test and sets a proportional threshold, keeping P_{FA} constant (Barkat, chapters 11 and 12).

CA-CFAR threshold factor
P_{FA}=\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{N}\right)^{-N}\;\Longrightarrow\;\alpha=N\left(P_{FA}^{-1/N}-1\right)

❓ Frequently asked

Why not a fixed threshold?Because P_{FA} depends on the noise power; if the noise doubles, false alarms jump.
What if a target sits in the reference cells?The threshold is pushed up and the target is masked; other CFAR variants are reviewed in section 11.3.2.

📌 Worked example with real numbers

CA-CFAR with N=16 reference cells and P_{FA}=10^{-2} in square-law noise: threshold factor \alpha=5.336 times the reference-cell mean; a 500 000-trial simulation gives P_{FA}=0.0099.

12Numerical verification and reportingall modules

🎓 Theory

A result is not finished until two independent methods agree: FFT with a direct sum, numerical integral with a closed form, formula with simulation. When reporting, state f_s, N, the window, the random seed and the library versions so others can reproduce it.

❓ Frequently asked

Why two methods?Because a single method can be wrong in the same direction as you (a 2\pi constant, an exponent sign, a 1/N normalisation).
What should be recorded to reproduce it?The experiment parameters, the random seed, and the NumPy/SciPy versions used.

📌 Worked example with real numbers

Every notebook in this course does exactly this: for example module 1 prints the deviation between FFT and direct sum and stops if it exceeds 10^{-9}.
